The importance of software quality assurance is well known. It helps identify bugs, prevent unexpected behavior and ensure that software works as intended.
Nevertheless, software failures may still occur, sometimes leading to devastating consequences.

Software is everywhere. It runs our phones, powers aircraft, controls medical equipment and guides spacecraft through space.
While a bug in an everyday application might cause a minor inconvenience, a failure in a [critical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_system) can result in serious financial losses, destroyed equipment or even loss of life.

Throughout history, various software failures have led to major disasters.
Let's take a look at some of the most notable incidents and what went wrong.

## Therac-25: Software that Killed

<img src="/therac-25.png" alt="Therac-25" className="max-w-64 mx-auto" />

In the 1980s, Therac-25, a radiation therapy machine developed by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL), was used to treat cancer patients. It was a computer-controlled medical accelerator designed to destroy cancer cells by delivering controlled doses of radiation while limiting damage to surrounding healthy tissue.

The machine supported two treatment modes. In electron mode, a beam of electrons was spread out and delivered directly to the patient to treat relatively shallow tumors. In X-ray mode, a much more intense electron beam was directed at a metal target to produce X-rays capable of treating deeper tumors. A rotating mechanism called a turntable positioned the appropriate components in the beam's path, while software controlled the treatment settings and monitored the machine's operation.

Between 1985 and 1987, the Therac-25 was involved in several known accidents in which patients received massive radiation overdoses, causing serious injuries and deaths.

### Behind the Failure

Unlike earlier models, the Therac-25 relied more heavily on software to maintain safety, rather than on independent hardware safeguards.
This made software defects particularly dangerous.
One such defect was a race condition, where the program's behavior depended on the timing of operations.

In one accident, a technician mistakenly selected X-ray mode and quickly changed it to electron mode.
Due to a software synchronization error, the machine failed to update its configuration correctly, leaving it set to produce a dangerously intense radiation beam.
As a result, the patient received a massive radiation overdose.

## Ariane 5: Software that Destroyed a Rocket

In 1996, the European Ariane 5 rocket was launched on its first flight.
Shortly after liftoff, things went terribly wrong, and the rocket was destroyed.

The surprising part? The failure was caused by software that had previously worked successfully.

### The Underlying Problem

The Ariane 5 reused software from its predecessor, Ariane 4.
One part of this software was responsible for calculations related to the rocket's alignment and horizontal velocity.
The problem was that Ariane 5 had different flight characteristics, including a much higher horizontal velocity.
A calculation that had worked for Ariane 4 produced a value that exceeded the range the software could handle.
This caused an integer overflow during conversion, triggering a chain of events that ultimately led to the rocket's destruction.

Notably, the problem had not been discovered during testing because the software had not been tested using the actual flight conditions of Ariane 5.

## Mars Climate Orbiter: Lost without a Trace

In December 1998, NASA launched the Mars Climate Orbiter, a spacecraft designed to study the Martian atmosphere and climate from orbit and serve as a communications relay for other Mars missions.

After traveling for approximately nine months, the spacecraft approached Mars in 1999.
However, instead of successfully entering its planned orbit, it disappeared behind the planet and never re-established contact.
Investigations indicated that the spacecraft had approached Mars at a dangerously low altitude and was eventually destroyed in its atmosphere.

### Behind the Failure

The main problem was surprisingly simple: two software systems were using different units of measurement.

One software system produced navigation-related data in imperial units (pound-seconds), while another expected metric units (newton-seconds).
This mismatch, involving a conversion factor of approximately 4.45, caused errors in trajectory calculations.

The spacecraft's asymmetric design also contributed to the problem.
Its thrusters fired more frequently than expected, and each firing slightly changed the spacecraft's trajectory.
Over time, the accumulated errors caused the spacecraft to drift away from its intended trajectory and approach Mars far too closely.

A seemingly simple mismatch in measurement units ultimately contributed to the loss of an entire spacecraft.
